How to Spot a Fake Invoice Scam

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Designed and built by Alex using modern tools, including AI. Published October 8, 2026.

A fake invoice scam is simple. Someone sends your business a bill for something you never ordered, or a real-looking bill with changed bank details, and hopes a busy person pays without checking. No hacking is involved. The scammer counts on routine and speed. This guide gives you a short routine that makes these bills easy to catch.

This is general guidance for small business owners, not legal advice. No routine is perfect, but a few steady habits make you a much harder target.

The two common versions

Most fake invoices fit one of two types. Knowing which one you have helps you check it.

1. The bill for something you never ordered

It comes as an email attachment, a mailed letter, or a phone call about an "overdue" account. It might be for office supplies, domain renewals, directory listings or software. The sender is not one of your real suppliers. They hope your bookkeeper assumes someone else ordered it.

2. The real supplier with new bank details

This one is harder to catch. An email seems to come from a vendor you really use and says "we have changed banks, please send future payments here." The address may be a lookalike, or the supplier's real mailbox may have been taken over. Either way, the money goes to the scammer.

Warning signs to look for

  • A sender address that is almost right. For example, billing@acme-supplies.example versus billing@acme-suppIies.example (with a capital I instead of an l). Click or tap the sender name to see the full address instead of just the display name.
  • A change in payment details. Any new account number, new payment method, or "please pay by gift card or wire" request is worth a pause.
  • No purchase order or reference you recognize. Real bills usually match something you ordered.
  • Pressure. A line like "pay today to avoid a penalty" is there to keep you from checking.
  • Generic wording. "Dear customer" or "Dear business owner" in place of your name or company name.
  • Odd attachments or links. A bill that asks you to open a file or sign in on a web page to "view the invoice" is a red flag.
  • Small mistakes. Wrong company name, wrong address, odd spelling, or a logo that looks stretched.

One warning sign alone may be harmless. If you see two or three, slow down.

Five steps to run before you pay any invoice

  1. Match it to something real. Can you point to the order, contract, or job this bill belongs to? If nobody on your team can say who ordered it, do not pay it yet.
  2. Check the sender. Look at the full email address. Compare it to an earlier, trusted email from the same supplier. If you can, start a new email to the supplier using the address you already have on file instead of replying to the suspicious message.
  3. Call to confirm any change. If the bill has new bank details, phone the supplier using a number from your own records or their official website. Do not use the phone number printed on the invoice or in the email. A scammer controls that number.
  4. Use a second person. The person who approves a payment should not be the only one who sees it. Even in a two-person company, ask the other person to take a quick look before you add a new payee.
  5. Write it down. Keep a short note of who you spoke to and when. It helps if anything goes wrong later.

Build a simple "two-person rule"

The most useful habit for a small business is a rule: any change to payment details must be confirmed by a phone call to a known number, and a second person must agree. Write it on one page and share it with everyone who touches bills, including part-time helpers and your bookkeeper.

It works because nobody has to spot a clever fake. The scam depends on a quick payment, and the rule makes that impossible without a check.

Safer habits for your inbox

  • Do not open attachments you were not expecting. If a bill claims to be from a supplier you use, check with them first.
  • Do not sign in to anything through a link in an invoice email. Go to the website by typing the address yourself.
  • Turn on two-step login for your business email so a scammer has a harder time taking over your mailbox. Our guide on turning on two-step login for business email shows how.
  • To learn more about spotting phishing, CISA has a short, plain-language page at cisa.gov.

What to do if you already paid a fake invoice

Stay calm and act quickly. You may feel embarrassed, but this happens to careful people too.

  1. Call your bank right away. Tell them you sent a payment to a fraudulent account and ask what they can do. Speed matters, and the bank can explain your options.
  2. Report it. In the United States you can report fraud at reportfraud.ftc.gov and, for internet-related financial crime, at ic3.gov.
  3. Check your email account. If a supplier email was sent from inside a real mailbox, one of the mailboxes involved may have been broken into. Our guide on what to do if your business email was hacked covers the next steps.
  4. Tell the real supplier. Let them know so they can warn other customers and check their own systems.

For anything involving contracts, disputes, or liability, speak with a licensed attorney. We cannot give legal advice.

A made-up example

Picture a small print shop. On a Friday afternoon, an email arrives that looks like it is from the paper supplier, billing@papersupply.example. It says the supplier has moved banks and asks that the next invoice be paid to a new account. The owner notices that the earlier emails came from accounts@paper-supply.example, a slightly different address. She does not reply. She calls the number saved in her phone, and the supplier says nothing has changed. The call takes two minutes, and the payment goes where it should.

The short version

Match every bill to a real order, check the sender, phone a known number for any change, and get a second person to look. Do those four things every time and fake invoices get much easier to catch.
