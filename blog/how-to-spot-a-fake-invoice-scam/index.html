How to Spot a Fake Invoice Scam

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Designed and built by Alex using modern tools, including AI. Published October 8, 2026.

A fake invoice scam is simple. Someone sends your business a bill for something you never ordered, or a real-looking bill with changed bank details, and hopes a busy person pays without checking. There is no hacking involved. The scammer is counting on routine and speed. This guide gives you a short routine that makes these bills easy to catch.

This is general guidance for small business owners, not legal advice. No routine is perfect, but a few steady habits make you a much harder target.

The two common versions

Most fake invoices fall into one of two groups. Knowing which one you are looking at helps you check it.

1. The bill for something you never ordered

This arrives as an email attachment, a mailed letter, or a phone call about an "overdue" account. It might be for office supplies, domain renewals, directory listings, or software. The sender is not one of your real suppliers. They hope your bookkeeper assumes someone else ordered it.

2. The real supplier with new bank details

This one is trickier. An email appears to come from a vendor you really use, and says "we have changed banks, please send future payments here." The email may be a lookalike address, or the supplier's real mailbox may have been taken over. Either way, the money goes to the scammer.

Warning signs to look for

  • A sender address that is almost right. For example, billing@acme-supplies.example versus billing@acme-suppIies.example (with a capital I instead of an l). Click or tap the sender name to see the full address, not just the display name.
  • A change in payment details. Any new account number, new payment method, or "please pay by gift card or wire" request deserves a pause.
  • No purchase order or reference you recognize. Real bills usually match something you ordered.
  • Pressure. Phrases like "pay today to avoid a penalty" are meant to stop you from checking.
  • Generic wording. "Dear customer" or "Dear business owner" instead of your name or company name.
  • Odd attachments or links. A bill that asks you to open a file or sign in on a web page to "view the invoice" is a red flag.
  • Small mistakes. Wrong company name, wrong address, odd spelling, or a logo that looks stretched.

One warning sign alone may be harmless. Two or three together mean you should slow down.

Your five-step check before paying any invoice

  1. Match it to something real. Can you point to the order, contract, or job this bill belongs to? If nobody on your team can say who ordered it, do not pay it yet.
  2. Check the sender. Look at the full email address. Compare it to an earlier, trusted email from the same supplier. If you can, start a brand-new email to the supplier using the address you already have on file, instead of replying to the suspicious message.
  3. Call to confirm any change. If the bill has new bank details, phone the supplier using a number from your own records or their official website. Do not use the phone number printed on the invoice or in the email, because a scammer controls that.
  4. Use a second person. The person who approves a payment should not be the only person who sees it. Even in a two-person company, ask the other person to take a quick look before any new payee is added.
  5. Write it down. Keep a short note of who you spoke to and when. If anything goes wrong later, you will be glad you did.

Build a simple "two-person rule"

The most useful habit for a small business is a rule that says: any change to payment details must be confirmed by a phone call to a known number, and a second person must agree. Write it on one page and share it with everyone who touches bills, including part-time helpers and your bookkeeper.

The rule works because it does not depend on anyone spotting a clever fake. It simply makes the scam's key step, a quick payment, impossible to complete without a check.

Safer habits for your inbox

  • Do not open attachments you were not expecting. If a bill claims to be from a supplier you use, check with them first.
  • Do not sign in to anything through a link in an invoice email. Go to the website by typing the address yourself.
  • Turn on two-step login for your business email so a scammer has a harder time taking over your mailbox. Our guide on turning on two-step login for business email walks through it.
  • Learn to recognize phishing more broadly. CISA has a short, plain-language page at cisa.gov.

What to do if you already paid a fake invoice

Stay calm and act quickly. You may feel embarrassed, but this happens to careful people.

  1. Call your bank right away. Tell them you sent a payment to a fraudulent account and ask what they can do. Speed matters, and the bank can tell you your options.
  2. Report it. In the United States you can report fraud at reportfraud.ftc.gov and, for internet-related financial crime, at ic3.gov.
  3. Check your email account. If a supplier email was faked from inside a real mailbox, one of the mailboxes involved may have been broken into. Our guide on what to do if your business email was hacked covers the next steps.
  4. Tell the real supplier. Let them know so they can warn other customers and check their own systems.

For anything involving contracts, disputes, or liability, speak with a licensed attorney. We cannot give legal advice.

A quick example, all made up

Imagine a small print shop. On a Friday afternoon, an email arrives that looks like it is from the paper supplier, billing@papersupply.example. It says the supplier has moved banks and asks that the next invoice be paid to a new account. The owner notices that the earlier emails came from accounts@paper-supply.example, a slightly different address. She does not reply. Instead she calls the number saved in her phone, and the supplier says nothing has changed. The call took two minutes, and it kept the payment where it belonged.

The short version

Match every bill to a real order. Check the sender. Phone a known number for any change. Get a second set of eyes. Those four habits, repeated every time, make fake invoices much easier to catch, without any special software.
