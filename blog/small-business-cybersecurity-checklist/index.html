Small Business Cybersecurity Checklist

By Alex Baldwin, Collar City Tech Help. Prepared with AI tools and checked before it's sent. Published October 8, 2026.

You do not need to be a technical expert to protect your business. Most of the useful steps are habits and settings, not expensive tools. This checklist walks through the basics in a sensible order. Work through it over a few weeks, tick things off, and come back every few months.

This is general guidance, not legal advice, and no checklist can guarantee you will never be hacked. The goal is to make your business a harder target and to recover faster if something goes wrong.

How to use this checklist

Print it or copy it into a note. Do not try to finish everything in a day. Start with Section 1, which gives the most protection for the least effort, and move down. Where a section has a longer guide, we link to it.

1. Lock down your email and logins

Email is the key to your other accounts, so start here.

  • Turn on two-step login for your business email. See how to turn on two-step login for business email. Google explains its version at support.google.com.
  • Turn on two-step login for your bank, payment tools, accounting software, website host, and social media.
  • Check that the recovery email and phone number on each important account are yours and current.
  • Give every employee their own login. Avoid shared accounts where you can.

2. Fix your passwords

  • Stop reusing passwords. Start with email and banking.
  • Use long, unique passwords, which are easier to manage with a password manager. Read whether small businesses need a password manager.
  • Never send passwords by email, text, or chat.
  • Change default passwords on anything new, such as your Wi-Fi router, printers, and cameras.
  • For general tips, see CISA's page on strong passwords.

3. Keep devices and software updated

  • Turn on automatic updates on every computer, phone, and tablet.
  • Update your web browser and the apps you use most often.
  • Update your Wi-Fi router occasionally. Check the maker's website or app.
  • Replace devices that no longer receive updates from their maker.
  • Use the security tools that come with your system, such as Windows Security or the Mac's built-in protections, and keep them switched on.

4. Back up your files and test the backup

  • List your important files and where they live.
  • Keep at least two copies, on different kinds of storage, with one stored off-site.
  • Make the backup automatic.
  • Keep one copy that is not always connected to your computer.
  • Restore three files every few months to prove it works.

Details are in our guide on how to back up your business computer.

5. Protect your money

  • Adopt a two-person rule: any change to payment or bank details must be confirmed by a phone call to a number you already had, and a second person agrees.
  • Match every invoice to a real order before paying.
  • Limit who can move money, and use separate approval and payment roles where possible.
  • Review bank statements regularly for anything you do not recognize.

See how to spot a fake invoice scam for a five-step routine.

6. Train yourself and your team

  • Spend twenty minutes together on the warning signs of suspicious emails: pressure, surprise attachments, odd sender addresses, and requests to change payment details.
  • Agree on what to do with a suspicious message: do not click, tell the owner, and delete it. A simple "report" habit is more useful than blame.
  • Show people how to check a link before clicking by hovering over it, or by going to the website directly.
  • Repeat the conversation whenever someone new joins.
  • CISA offers short guides on phishing at cisa.gov.

7. Manage who has access

  • Make a simple list of the tools and accounts your business uses and who has access to each.
  • Give people only the access they need for their job.
  • When someone leaves, remove their access and change shared passwords on the same day.
  • Review the list twice a year and remove anything you no longer use.

8. Secure your Wi-Fi and office devices

  • Change the router's admin password from the default.
  • Use a strong Wi-Fi password and the newest security option your router supports.
  • Set up a separate guest network for customers and visitors.
  • Lock your screen when you step away, and set devices to lock automatically.
  • Turn on device encryption and "find my device" features on laptops and phones.

9. Take care of your email domain and website

  • Ask your email provider to confirm that SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are set up for your domain. These are public records that make it harder for others to fake email from your business.
  • Keep your website and any plug-ins updated, and use a strong login with two-step verification for the admin area.
  • Make sure your domain registration is in your own name, with a current email address, and set to renew.

10. Have a plan for when something goes wrong

  • Write down who to call first: your bank, your email provider, and a trusted IT person if you have one.
  • Keep the main steps handy. Our guide on what to do if your business email was hacked is a good starting point.
  • Know where to report problems: reportfraud.ftc.gov for fraud and scams, and ic3.gov for internet-related crime.
  • Keep contact details for your key suppliers outside your email, so you can reach them if your inbox is unavailable.

A sensible order if you are short on time

  1. Two-step login on email and banking.
  2. Unique passwords for your most important accounts.
  3. Automatic updates everywhere.
  4. Automatic backups with one tested restore.
  5. A two-person rule for payment changes.
  6. A twenty-minute team talk about suspicious emails.

These six steps cover the areas where small businesses most often get stuck, and none requires special equipment.

Keep it going

Set a reminder to go through this list every three to six months, and after any big change such as a new hire, a new tool, or a new computer. Security is not a one-time project. Small, regular check-ins keep it manageable.
