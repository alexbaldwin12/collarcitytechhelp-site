Do Small Businesses Need a Password Manager?

By Alex Baldwin, Collar City Tech Help. AI-assisted, reviewed by a UAlbany cybersecurity student. Published October 8, 2026.

Short answer: for most small businesses, yes, a password manager is worth having. Longer answer: it depends on how many accounts you have, how many people share them, and how you handle passwords today. This article explains what a password manager is, the honest trade-offs, and how to set one up in an afternoon.

This is general guidance, not legal advice or a guarantee. Any tool works best when paired with other habits.

What a password manager actually does

A password manager is an app that stores your logins in an encrypted vault. You remember one strong main password, and the app remembers the rest. It can create long random passwords for each account, fill them in for you on websites, and warn you when you have reused one.

Think of it as a locked filing cabinet for your logins, with a key that only you hold. Most work on computers, phones, and tablets, so your vault goes with you.

Why reusing passwords is the real problem

Most people have far more accounts than they can memorize. The natural shortcut is to reuse the same password, or small variations of it. The risk is that when one website is breached, the stolen password may be tried on your email, your bank, and your other tools. One weak spot can open many doors.

A password manager removes the shortcut. Because it remembers everything, every account can have its own long, random password that you never need to type or recall.

Signs your business would benefit

  • You reuse passwords, even a few of them.
  • Passwords are kept in a notebook, a sticky note, a spreadsheet, or a shared document.
  • Several people share a login for social media, a supplier portal, or accounting software.
  • You are not sure who still has access after an employee leaves.
  • You often click "forgot password" because you cannot remember what you used.

If two or more of these sound familiar, a password manager will probably make life easier as well as safer.

Honest trade-offs

A password manager is not magic, and it has costs:

  • Everything depends on your main password. If you forget it, recovery can be difficult. If someone learns it, they may reach your vault. This is why you should pick a long passphrase and turn on two-step login for the manager itself.
  • There is a small learning curve. The first week feels slower while you move accounts over.
  • Some paid plans cost money per user. There are free and low-cost options, and your choice should fit your budget.
  • It does not stop every scam. It will not fill a password on a fake website that does not match the saved address, which helps, but you still need to watch for phishing.

How to choose one

Look for these basics rather than a brand name:

  1. It works on all the devices your team uses.
  2. It supports two-step login for the vault.
  3. It lets you share logins with specific people without sending the password in an email or text.
  4. It has a way to remove a person's access when they leave.
  5. It has a plan that fits your budget, whether free or paid. Bitwarden, for example, offers a free tier and business plans, and publishes clear instructions at bitwarden.com/help. Many others exist, so compare a few and choose what suits you.

Setting it up, step by step

  1. Create your vault. Sign up with your business email and choose a long main password: a sentence of four or five unrelated words works well. Do not reuse it anywhere. Write it down once and keep that paper somewhere locked.
  2. Turn on two-step login for the vault. This is the most important setting. If you need a refresher on how that works, read how to turn on two-step login for business email.
  3. Install the app and browser extension on your computer and phone, using the official download pages.
  4. Start with your most important accounts. Begin with your business email, your bank, your payment tools, and your website host. For each, open the account's password change page, let the manager generate a new long password, and save it.
  5. Work through the rest over a week or two. Each time you sign in somewhere, let the manager save or update the entry. You do not have to do everything in one day.
  6. Delete old password lists. Once everything is in the vault, remove the spreadsheet or notebook page. Leaving it around defeats the purpose.

For general advice on creating strong passwords, CISA has a clear page at cisa.gov.

Sharing logins with your team

Shared logins are where small businesses often get into trouble. A good setup works like this:

  • Give each person their own vault and their own login wherever the tool allows separate users.
  • For tools that only offer one login, put it in a shared folder in the manager so the people who need it can use it without ever seeing or typing the password.
  • When someone leaves, remove their access to the shared folder and change the shared passwords that they could reach.

Never send passwords by email, text, or chat. If a customer or colleague sends you one, do not copy it elsewhere, and suggest they change it.

What if a password manager feels like too much?

If you are a one-person business with only a handful of accounts, you might start smaller: use a unique long passphrase for your email and bank, turn on two-step login on both, and keep a short written list in a locked place. That is better than reusing passwords. Many people find that once they feel comfortable with that, a manager is an easy upgrade.

Where this fits with everything else

A password manager is one layer. It works best next to two-step login, regular updates, and tested backups. Our small business cybersecurity checklist puts all of these in one list so you can see what to do first.

The short version

If you reuse passwords, share logins, or struggle to keep track of accounts, a password manager is a practical, affordable fix. Set one up with a strong main password, add two-step login, move your key accounts first, and retire the old lists. Expect a small bump in the first week and a calmer process after that.
