Restaurant Wi-Fi Security: Set Up a Guest Network

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Published October 8, 2026.

If you run a restaurant or cafe in Troy, Albany or anywhere in the Capital Region, free wifi is part of the deal. Customers expect it. What owners often skip is thinking about what else that wifi connects to. When guests, the office laptop, the register and the security camera all share one network, a problem on one device can reach the others.

The fix is a separate guest network. Most routers sold in the last several years can do this, and it usually takes about an hour. Router menus differ by brand, so treat the names below as a guide to what to look for. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

Why a separate guest network matters

Your guests bring phones and laptops you know nothing about. Some may be infected without the owner knowing. If those devices sit on the same network as your office computer, they can try to see shared folders, printers and other equipment. A guest network gives visitors internet access and that's all.

It helps with a practical problem too. If your wifi password is written on the chalkboard, anyone who has ever eaten with you has it. On a guest network, that password only opens the guest door.

Step 1: Find your router and log in to it

Look for the box your internet provider installed, or one you bought yourself. The login address and default admin details are often printed on a label on the router. Open the address in a web browser on a computer that is connected to the router.

If the admin password is still the factory one from the label, change it now to a long, unique password. Store it in a password manager or a locked drawer, not on a sticky note by the register. Our guide on password managers for small businesses explains the easy way to do that.

Step 2: Update the router

Look for a section called Firmware, Software Update or Administration. If an update is available, install it. Updates fix known problems in the router itself. If your provider manages the router remotely, ask them whether it is kept up to date.

Step 3: Turn on the guest network

Look for a setting called Guest Network, Guest Wi-Fi or Guest SSID. Switch it on and then set:

  • A name customers will recognize. Your business name plus the word Guest works well. Do not use the same name as your private network.
  • A password. Make it different from your private wifi password. Something short and readable is fine, because it only protects the guest side.
  • Encryption. Choose WPA3 if your router offers it. If not, choose WPA2. Avoid any option labeled Open, WEP or WPA only, unless you have a specific reason.

Step 4: Block guests from your own equipment

Many routers have a checkbox on the guest settings page with a name like Client Isolation, AP Isolation or Allow guests to access my local network. You want guests isolated from each other and from your private network. Make sure local network access for guests is off.

If your router has no guest option at all, that is a good reason to replace it. A basic small business router or a home mesh system with guest support is not a large expense, and it is worth asking your internet provider what they offer.

Step 5: Put the right devices on the right network

Here is a simple way to sort them.

  • Guest network: customers, and maybe a tablet for the staff to stream music.
  • Private network: office computer, printer, cameras, and anything that handles orders or money.

Card readers and point of sale systems vary. Some use their own cellular connection, and some use your wifi or a wired connection. Check the setup instructions from your payment provider, or ask them directly, before you move any payment device. Don't guess on this one. If you accept cards, your provider can tell you what network setup they expect. This article cannot tell you whether you meet any card industry rules.

Step 6: Test it

  1. On your phone, connect to the guest network.
  2. Open a website to confirm that the internet works.
  3. Try to open your office printer's page or reach a shared folder. It should not work.
  4. Switch back to your private network and confirm that your normal devices still work.

If something on the private side stopped working, undo the last change and ask your internet provider for help. Write down what you changed so you can reverse it.

Step 7: Keep it tidy

  • Change the guest password every few months, and the private password whenever someone with access leaves.
  • Remove old devices from your router's connected-device list. If you do not recognize one, find out what it is.
  • Turn off remote administration unless you truly need it. This setting lets someone reach your router's login page from outside your building.
  • Put a reminder on the calendar to look for router updates twice a year.

What about a splash page and terms?

Some restaurants add a welcome page that customers click through. That is optional. If you want one, check whether your router offers it before paying for a separate service. Whether you need wording about acceptable use is a question for your own lawyer, because the answer depends on your situation.

A quick Saturday-morning checklist

  • Router admin password changed from the default.
  • Router updates installed.
  • Guest network on, with its own name and password.
  • WPA3 or WPA2 selected.
  • Guests blocked from the private network.
  • Office computer, printer and cameras on the private side.
  • Payment devices set up the way your provider says.
