What to Do If Your Business Email Was Hacked

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Designed and built by Alex using modern tools, including AI. Published October 8, 2026.

Finding out that someone else may have been in your business email is stressful. Take a breath. You can limit most of the damage by working through a list in order. This guide is for non-technical owners and is general guidance, not legal advice. If a serious loss or legal question is involved, contact your bank, and a licensed attorney where appropriate.

Signs your email may have been hacked

  • Friends, customers, or suppliers say they got strange messages from you.
  • You see sent emails that you did not write.
  • Your password stopped working, or you were signed out unexpectedly.
  • You get password reset emails you did not request.
  • There are new forwarding rules, signatures, or filters you did not create.
  • Your provider sends a "new sign-in" or "unusual activity" alert for a place or device you do not recognize.

Any of these can have a harmless explanation, but take them seriously until you've checked.

First hour: stop the access

1. Use a device you trust

If you think your computer may be infected, use a different device, such as a phone or a family member's computer, for the next steps. Do not click links in any suspicious messages.

2. Change the email password

Go to your email provider's official sign-in page by typing the address yourself, not from a link in a message. Change the password to a long, new one that you have never used anywhere else. If you can no longer sign in, use the provider's account recovery page. For Google accounts, see Google Account Help (search for "hacked account"). For Microsoft accounts, search for "recover a hacked account" on support.microsoft.com.

3. Sign out other sessions

Most providers have a security page that lists where you are signed in. Look for a "sign out of all devices" or "sign out everywhere" option and use it. This kicks out anyone who is still connected.

4. Turn on two-step login

If it is not already on, turn it on now. Our guide to turning on two-step login for business email has the steps.

Next: look for changes the intruder left behind

People who break into email often change settings so they can keep watching, even after you change the password. Check each of these:

  1. Forwarding. Look in your settings for any rule that forwards mail to an address you do not know. Delete it.
  2. Filters and rules. Look for rules that move, hide, or delete certain messages, such as ones containing the word "invoice" or "bank." Remove any you did not make.
  3. Recovery details. Check the backup email address and phone number on the account. Make sure they are yours.
  4. Connected apps. In your security settings, look for third-party apps with access to your account. Remove any you do not recognize.
  5. Delegates and signatures. Check whether someone else was given access to your mailbox, and review your email signature for changes.
  6. Sent and deleted folders. Skim these for messages you did not send. They tell you who might have been contacted.

Check your computer

If the password was stolen because of a malicious program on your computer, changing it from that same computer may not help. Run a full scan with your computer's built-in security tools (Windows Security or the equivalent on your system). Make sure the system is up to date. If you are unsure, ask a trusted local IT professional to look at it. We do not log in to or test client systems.

Then: protect the other accounts

If you reused your email password anywhere else, change it there too. Prioritize in this order:

  1. Your bank and payment tools
  2. Accounting software
  3. Your website, domain, and social media accounts
  4. Cloud storage and shared documents
  5. Everything else

Because email is used for password resets, an intruder with access to your inbox may be able to request resets for other accounts. Remember to give each account a different password. A password manager makes that much easier.

Contact your bank if money may be involved

If the intruder could have seen invoices, changed payment details, or sent payment requests, call your bank using the number on your bank card or official website. Explain what happened. Ask what they recommend about recent payments and account security. Do not wait on this, because timing can matter.

If you suspect that a fake invoice was paid, our guide on spotting a fake invoice scam includes extra steps.

Warn the people who may have been affected

Tell your contacts honestly and briefly. You can send a short note from a safe account, or call your most important contacts. Here is an example message, using made-up names:

Hello, this is Sam from Maple Street Plumbing. Our email account was accessed by someone else between Monday and Wednesday. If you got a message from us asking you to open a file, click a link, or change payment details, please do not act on it. If you are unsure about any message, call us on our regular number to check. We are sorry for the trouble.

Keep the tone calm and factual. If you hold customer information and the intruder may have seen it, the law may require you to notify people or agencies in some situations. Because that depends on your state and your type of business, speak with a licensed attorney rather than relying on a general article like this one.

Report it

  • Report fraud and scams at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
  • Report internet-related crime, especially if money was lost, at ic3.gov.
  • Tell your email provider using their reporting or support channel.

Keep notes

Write down what you saw, the dates and times, what you changed, and who you contacted. Screenshots help. These notes help when you talk to your bank, when you file a report, and when you try to remember details later.

After the emergency: make it harder next time

  • Keep two-step login on for every account that offers it.
  • Use unique passwords stored in a password manager.
  • Be cautious with unexpected attachments and sign-in links. CISA has a helpful page on recognizing phishing at cisa.gov.
  • Keep tested backups of important files.
  • Review our small business cybersecurity checklist and work through it over a few weeks.

The short version

Change the password from a trusted device, sign out everywhere, turn on two-step login, remove forwarding rules and unknown apps, check your computer, secure your other accounts, call your bank if money is involved, warn your contacts, and report it. You don't need to be an expert. Take the list one step at a time.
