How to Turn On Two-Step Login for Business Email

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Designed and built by Alex using modern tools, including AI. Published October 8, 2026.

Your business email is the key to almost everything else. Password reset links, invoices, customer messages and bank notices all land there. If someone gets in, they can often get into your other accounts too. Two-step login is one of the best single changes you can make, and it usually takes about 15 minutes.

This guide is general guidance for non-technical business owners. Menus change from time to time, so if a screen looks a little different from what we describe, follow the official help pages linked below.

What two-step login means

Normally you sign in with one thing, your password. Two-step login (also called two-factor authentication, 2FA, or multi-factor authentication) adds a second check. After you type your password, you confirm something only you have, like a code from an app on your phone or a tap on a prompt.

If someone learns or guesses your password, they still cannot get in without your phone. It does not make an account unbreakable, but it shuts the most common way in.

Before you start

  • Have your phone nearby and unlocked.
  • Know which email service you use. Most small businesses use Google Workspace or Gmail, Microsoft 365 or Outlook, or email that comes with their website host.
  • Find your recovery details. Make sure the backup email address and phone number on the account are current and yours.
  • Tell your team. If you manage other people's accounts, tell them a change is coming so a new prompt does not catch them off guard.

Which second step should you pick?

You will usually see several options. Here they are, roughly from stronger to more basic:

  1. A passkey or a physical security key. Very strong, and easy to use once it is set up.
  2. An authenticator app. An app on your phone shows a short code that changes every half minute, or asks you to approve a sign-in. It is a solid everyday choice.
  3. A text message code. Better than nothing and fine to start with, though an app is usually the better pick.

If you are unsure, pick an authenticator app or the prompt option your email provider suggests. You can add a backup method later.

Google (Gmail and Google Workspace)

  1. Go to your Google Account by signing in at myaccount.google.com.
  2. Choose Security from the left-hand menu.
  3. Find 2-Step Verification and select it.
  4. Choose Get started and follow the prompts. Google will ask you to confirm your password and then pick a second step, such as a prompt on your phone.
  5. When it finishes, add a backup option, such as a second phone number or backup codes.

Google walks through each screen on its own help page: Turn on 2-Step Verification. If you use Google Workspace, your administrator may need to allow or require it for everyone. If that is you, look for the 2-Step Verification settings in the security area of the Admin console, and check Google's help for the current wording.

Microsoft (Outlook and Microsoft 365)

  1. Sign in to your Microsoft account and open the Security area.
  2. Look for Advanced security options or Two-step verification.
  3. Choose to turn it on and follow the prompts to add the Microsoft Authenticator app or another method.
  4. Save the recovery code Microsoft gives you in a safe place.

Work and school accounts managed by an administrator may be set up differently. Microsoft keeps current instructions at support.microsoft.com; search there for "two-step verification" or "multifactor authentication" for your kind of account.

Other email providers

If your email comes with your website host or another provider, look for words like "security," "two-factor," or "2FA" in the account settings. If you cannot find it, search the provider's help pages or ask their support team: "How do I turn on two-step login for my email account?" CISA has a plain-language overview of why this matters and the different types at cisa.gov.

Set up backups so you don't lock yourself out

The most common problem with two-step login is losing the second step, usually through a lost or replaced phone. Five minutes now prevents that:

  • Print or save the backup codes your provider offers and keep them somewhere safe, like a locked drawer or your password manager.
  • Add a second method, such as a second phone number or a second device.
  • When you buy a new phone, move your authenticator app over before you wipe the old one. Many apps have a transfer or backup feature.

Do not share codes with anyone, including someone who says they are from your provider. A real provider will never ask you to read out a code.

Test it

Open a private or incognito browser window, sign in to your email, and confirm that you are asked for the second step. Then sign out. It is much easier to find a problem now than in the middle of a busy day.

What if you manage a team?

Make it a company rule that everyone with a business email uses two-step login. Start with the owner and anyone who handles money or customer data, then move on to everyone else. Give people a short written guide, and sit with anyone who feels unsure. Remove the accounts of people who leave the business the same day they leave.

Two-step login also goes well with a password manager. Read whether small businesses need a password manager for the next step.

Common questions

Will it slow us down?

A little, at first. Many services only ask for the second step on a new device or every so often, so day-to-day use stays quick.

Is it still worth doing if a text message is the only option?

Yes. A text code is better than a password alone. Switch to an app later if you can.

What if I suspect someone already got in?

Turning on two-step login helps from now on, but it will not remove someone who is already inside. See what to do if your business email was hacked.

The short version

Open your email account's security settings, find 2-Step Verification or two-step verification, choose an app or prompt, save your backup codes, and test it. Fifteen minutes puts a second lock on your inbox. No single step makes an account hack-proof, but this one is well worth doing.
