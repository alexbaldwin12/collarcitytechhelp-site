Is Your Small Business Website Secure? Troy, NY Checklist

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Designed and built by Alex using modern tools, including AI. Published October 8, 2026.

If you run a shop, office, or service business in Troy, Albany, or anywhere in the Capital Region, your website is often the first thing a new customer sees. Most owners set it up once and never look at it again. This checklist takes about 20 minutes, and you don't need a technical background.

This is general guidance, not legal advice, and no checklist can promise you will never be hacked. We're just looking for the easy gaps so you can close them.

1. Look for the padlock (HTTPS)

Open your site in a browser. The address should start with https:// and show a padlock. If you see "Not secure," visitors see it too. Most hosting companies offer a free certificate. Ask your host to turn it on, then check that http:// sends people to the https:// version.

2. Check who can log in

  • List everyone who has an admin login to your website, your host, and your domain account. Remove anyone who no longer needs it, such as a past web designer.
  • Use a long, unique password for each login. A password manager makes this easy. See whether small businesses need a password manager.
  • Turn on two-step login wherever it is offered. Our guide on two-step login for business email shows the idea.

3. Keep the site and its add-ons updated

If your site runs on WordPress, Wix, Squarespace, or a similar builder, updates matter. Old plug-ins and themes are a common way into a site. Turn on automatic updates if your platform offers them, and delete plug-ins you no longer use.

4. Make sure you can get it back

Ask your host whether they keep backups, how often, and how you restore one. Keep your own copy of your key pages, photos, and logo too. For the bigger picture, read how to back up your business computer.

5. Check your forms and what they collect

Only ask visitors for what you need. Do not ask for passwords, card numbers, or ID numbers through a contact form. Send payments through a trusted payment provider's own checkout page instead.

6. Look at your email setup

Scammers often fake email from your domain name. Three public settings, called SPF, DKIM and DMARC, help email providers tell real mail from fake. Your email provider or web host can tell you whether they are set up. Our Security Scorecard checks these public records for your domain.

7. Know who to call and what you would do

Write down your host's support contact, where your domain is registered, and who has the logins. If something looks wrong, such as pages you did not make, a warning from Google, or strange emails sent from your address, you won't be hunting for details.
