How to Spot Fake Invoice Scams: A Routine for Capital Region Businesses

By Alex Baldwin, Collar City Tech Help. Published October 9, 2026.

Invoices are a normal part of running a business in Troy, Albany, Schenectady or anywhere in the Capital Region. That is exactly why fake ones work. A bill for something small, sent to someone busy, often gets paid before anyone asks who ordered it. This post gives you a short routine to run before you pay anything you did not expect. It is general guidance, not legal advice.

What the official sources say to look for

We built this list from public guidance by federal agencies rather than from guesswork.

The Federal Trade Commission has warned small businesses that scammers send bills for things nobody ordered, such as tech support, domain registration or search engine services. Often an unfamiliar invoice reaches a staff member who does not handle purchasing. The FTC's advice is to have staff look closely at every invoice and to set clear rules for approving purchases from the vendors you really use. See the FTC's consumer alert, Run a small business? Pay your bills, not scammers.

The FTC's page on how to recognize and avoid phishing scams adds that warning signs include unfamiliar invoices, links to make a payment, and invitations to click a link to update payment details. It also notes that legitimate companies generally do not email or text links to update payment information.

CISA lists four signs of phishing on its page about how to recognize and report phishing: pressure tactics, requests for personal or financial information, untrusted shortened links, and incorrect email addresses or links, such as a misspelled domain name. CISA also points out that poor grammar is no longer a reliable clue, so a clean, polished message can still be fake.

The red flags, in plain English

  • You do not recognize the company or the purchase. If nobody can say who ordered it, do not pay it.
  • A deadline or a threat. Words like past due, final notice or service will be shut off are meant to rush you.
  • A payment link you did not ask for. Go to the vendor's site by typing the address yourself instead.
  • New payment details. A message that says a supplier has a new bank account deserves a phone call before anything else.
  • An address that is almost right. Read the sender's domain letter by letter. One swapped letter is a common trick.
  • An attachment you did not expect. Do not open it just to see what it is.
  • A request for logins, bank numbers or other private details. A real invoice does not need your password.

A five-minute routine before you pay

  1. Match it to a real purchase. Look for a purchase order, a quote, an email from your own side, or a note from whoever handles buying. No match means stop.
  2. Compare it to the last real invoice from that vendor. Check the amount range, the payment method, the sender and the formatting.
  3. Do not use the contact details on the invoice. CISA's advice is to contact the company through its verified website or a number you already know. Use the number from a past invoice or your contract.
  4. Look up the company you do not know. The FTC suggests searching the company name with words like review, scam or complaint.
  5. Ask a second person for anything unusual. Even a quick glance from a partner or bookkeeper catches mistakes.
  6. Pay only through your normal method. If you are not sure, wait. A real vendor will understand a one-day delay.

Make it a team habit

Fake invoices often land on whoever opens the mail or the shared inbox, not on the owner. Tell everyone, including part-timers, who is allowed to approve a bill. A short written rule works: no invoice gets paid until someone matches it to a real order. If your team handles money by email, you may also want to read how to spot a fake invoice scam and our post on changed-payment-detail scams for contractors.

If you think you already paid one

  1. Call your bank right away and ask what can be done about the payment. Speed matters.
  2. Do not reply to the sender or click anything else in the message.
  3. If you clicked a link or opened an attachment, the FTC advises updating your security software and running a scan. If you shared a Social Security, card or bank account number, the FTC points you to IdentityTheft.gov for steps based on what was lost.
  4. Change the password on anything you typed in, and turn on two-step login. Our post on what to do if your business email was hacked can help.

Where to report it

  • The FTC: ReportFraud.ftc.gov takes reports of fake invoices and other scams.
  • The FBI's IC3: ic3.gov is for internet crime, especially when money was sent.
  • The Anti-Phishing Working Group: the FTC suggests forwarding phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org.
  • New York State: the Attorney General has a consumer complaint page with forms by category. Pick the one that best fits what happened.

Reporting does not guarantee your money comes back, but it helps investigators spot patterns, and a record helps if your bank or insurer asks questions later.
