How to Back Up Your Business Computer

By Alex Baldwin, Collar City Tech Help. AI-assisted, reviewed by a UAlbany cybersecurity student. Published October 8, 2026.

If your business computer stopped working tomorrow, what would you lose? Customer lists, invoices, photos, contracts, and years of small details. A backup is a second copy of your files, kept somewhere else, so one accident does not erase everything. This guide shows you how to set one up in plain steps.

Files can be lost for many ordinary reasons: a spilled coffee, a dead hard drive, a stolen laptop, a mistaken delete, or ransomware, which is a type of attack that locks your files. A good backup helps with all of them. This is general guidance; no system is perfect, which is why we also talk about testing.

Step 1: Decide what needs to be backed up

Start by making a quick list. Ask yourself, "If this vanished, would it hurt my business?" Common examples:

  • Documents, spreadsheets, and contracts
  • Accounting and invoicing files
  • Customer and supplier lists
  • Photos, logos, and marketing material
  • Email archives, if you keep any on your computer
  • Anything that is only stored on one machine

Also note where these things live. Some may be in a Documents folder, others on the desktop, others in a program's own folder. Gathering your important files into a few known folders makes backups easier.

Step 2: Learn the 3-2-1 idea

A well-known rule of thumb for backups is called 3-2-1:

  • 3 copies of your important files: the original plus two backups.
  • 2 different kinds of storage, such as your computer and an external drive, or a drive and the cloud.
  • 1 copy kept somewhere else, away from your office, such as cloud storage or a drive stored at another location.

You do not need to do this perfectly on day one. Even two copies is much better than one. The idea is to avoid having everything in one place or on one device.

Step 3: Choose your backup methods

Option A: Cloud backup

A cloud backup service copies your files over the internet to a secure online location. It usually runs quietly in the background. It is helpful because the copy is off-site, so a fire, theft, or flood at your office does not touch it. Make sure whichever service you choose protects its account with two-step login, and check its pricing and storage limits before committing.

Option B: An external drive

An external hard drive is a small box you plug into your computer. It is inexpensive, quick, and under your control. Its weaknesses are that it can be forgotten, damaged, or stolen along with your computer if you leave it plugged in and sitting next to it.

Option C: Built-in tools

Windows and Mac computers have backup features built in. On a Mac, the feature is called Time Machine; Apple explains it at support.apple.com (search for "Time Machine"). On Windows, look for "Backup" or "File History" in settings, and see support.microsoft.com for current instructions for your version. These work well with an external drive.

A practical combination

For many small offices, a good mix is one cloud backup that runs automatically, plus one external drive that you connect on a regular schedule and then store away from the computer. That gives you different kinds of storage and an off-site copy.

Step 4: Set it to run automatically

The best backup is the one that happens without you remembering. Whatever method you use, find the setting for automatic or scheduled backups and turn it on. If you rely on plugging in a drive by hand, put a repeating reminder on your calendar, such as every Friday afternoon, and treat it like locking the door.

Also turn on automatic updates for your computer and backup software so problems get fixed without you having to chase them.

Step 5: Keep one copy disconnected

Ransomware and similar problems can reach any drive that is connected to your computer, including some cloud folders that sync every change. A copy that is not always connected gives you a safe fallback. For example, plug in an external drive, run the backup, then unplug it and put it in a drawer or at home. Some cloud services also keep older versions of files; look for "version history" or "file recovery" in the service's settings.

Step 6: Test your backup

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that matters most. A backup you have never tested is a hope, not a plan. Here is a simple way to test it:

  1. Pick three files from different folders, including one that you changed recently.
  2. Open your backup and find those files.
  3. Restore them to a different folder, such as a new folder on your desktop called "restore test."
  4. Open each restored file and check that it works and looks right.
  5. Write the date down, and repeat every few months.

If anything fails, you have learned something while the stakes are low. Fix it now rather than later.

Step 7: Protect the backup itself

  • Use a strong, unique password on any backup account, and turn on two-step login. Our guide to two-step login shows how.
  • If your drive holds customer information, consider encrypting it. Windows and Mac both have built-in disk encryption options explained on their support sites.
  • Keep drives somewhere safe, such as a locked drawer.
  • Never share backup passwords by email or text. A password manager is a good place to store them.

What about phones and other devices?

Do not forget phones and tablets, which often hold photos, contacts, and authenticator apps. iPhones and Android phones both have built-in backup options. Turn them on and confirm the last backup date in your phone's settings.

Build it into your routine

A simple schedule might look like this: automatic cloud backup every day, an external drive backup every week, and a restore test every three months. Put the test on your calendar. If you have employees, write the plan on one page so anyone can follow it. The CISA site has more everyday safety guidance at cisa.gov/secure-our-world.

The short version

List what matters, keep at least two copies on different kinds of storage with one off-site, make it automatic, keep one copy disconnected, and test a restore.
