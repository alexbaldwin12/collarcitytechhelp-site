Small Business Cybersecurity in Troy, NY: Where to Start

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help. Published October 9, 2026.

If you run a business in Troy, you probably wear every hat. You are the owner, the scheduler, the person who answers the phone and, by default, the IT department. Security tends to land at the bottom of the list because it feels huge and technical. It doesn't have to. This post is a starting plan you can finish over a few evenings.

If you want a longer reference list afterward, our small business cybersecurity checklist covers more ground. This one is about where to begin. It is general guidance, not legal advice, and no plan can promise you will never be hacked. The aim is to make the easy doors harder to open.

Step 1: Write down what you actually use

You cannot protect what you have forgotten about. Grab a notebook or a blank document and list these four things:

  • Accounts. Business email, bank, payroll, accounting software, your website login, social media, Google Business Profile, any booking or ordering tool.
  • Devices. Laptops, phones, tablets, the register or card reader, the office printer, cameras.
  • People. Who has a login to what? Include the part-timer who left last spring.
  • Where the important files live. Customer records, invoices, photos, anything you would hate to lose.

Don't write passwords in this list. Just note which accounts exist and who can get into them. That one page shows you what to fix first.

Step 2: Lock down email first

Your business email is the master key. Most other accounts can send a password reset to it, so whoever controls your inbox can often get into everything else. Start there.

  • Give the account a long, unique password.
  • Turn on two-step login. Google explains how at Google's two-step verification page, and Microsoft covers it on its multifactor authentication page.
  • Check your forwarding rules. If you see a rule that sends copies of your mail somewhere you do not recognize, that deserves a closer look.

Our walkthrough on turning on two-step login for business email has the steps in plain language.

Step 3: Use a password manager for everything else

Reusing one password across accounts is the habit that causes the most trouble, because one leak opens many doors. A password manager creates and remembers a different password for each account, so you only memorize one. If you are new to the idea, read do small businesses need a password manager? and then try Bitwarden's getting started guide. Move your most important accounts over first: email, bank, accounting and your website.

If staff share a login for something, give each person their own where the tool allows it. That way, when someone leaves, you change one access instead of everyone's.

Step 4: Update and back up

Two dull habits do most of the work.

  • Updates. Turn on automatic updates for computers and phones, and restart now and then so they actually install. Apple explains it at Apple's software update page and Microsoft at the Windows Update FAQ.
  • Backups. Keep a copy of your important files somewhere other than the computer they live on, and try restoring one file to be sure it works. Our guide to backing up your business computer walks through it.

Step 5: Teach the team one habit

The most common way into a small business is still a message that looks normal and asks someone to click, pay or log in. Agree on one rule with everyone: if a message asks for money, a password or a changed payment detail, confirm it using a phone number you already had. CISA's page on how to recognize and report phishing is a good five-minute read to share with your staff.

What this looks like around Troy

Every business has a different weak spot. These are hypothetical examples of how the plan can fit your shop.

Example: a downtown cafe. The biggest items are the shared tablet and the wifi. Give the tablet its own login, keep customer wifi on a guest network, and read our guide to restaurant wifi security. More ideas are on the restaurants and cafes page.

Example: a small dental or medical office. Client or patient information makes email and shared computers the priority. Two-step login, locked screens and a clear rule about who can see what come first. Whether any particular rule applies to your office is a question for your own advisor, and our dental and medical offices page explains what we can and cannot help with.

Example: a contractor working out of a truck. The phone is the office. Lock it, update it, and set up two-step login on the email account that sends estimates and invoices. Be wary of any message that says payment details have changed. See the contractors and trades page.

Example: a retail shop on a busy block. Online orders, the card reader and social media accounts are all doorways. Give each its own strong password, and keep a short list of who can post to your accounts. The retail shops page has more.

How to pace yourself

Do Step 1 this week. Do Step 2 the next evening you have half an hour. Spread the rest over a month. You don't need it perfect, just done. If you only get through email and updates, you are already ahead of where you started.
