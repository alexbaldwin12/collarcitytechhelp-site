A plain-English security check for small dental and medical offices

By Alex Baldwin, Collar City Tech Help, Troy, NY. Last updated 2026-10-08.

A small practice holds a lot of private information. Names, phone numbers, appointment details and insurance paperwork pass through the front desk, the back office and the email inbox every day. People trust you with that.

Most small offices do not have an IT department. They have a front-desk workstation, a few shared computers, a provider who handles email on the side, and a backup drive somebody set up once. This page explains what our Security Scorecard looks at in that setting, and what it does not.

What the Security Scorecard checks for dental and medical offices

The Security Scorecard is a $79 report. You complete a 10-minute online questionnaire, we run public checks on your office email domain, and you get a graded report with a fix list in plain English. It is shaped by your answers, so a two-chair practice and a larger clinic get different advice.

  • Client and patient information in email. We ask whether you store customer records and whether staff get suspicious messages. The report explains why sensitive details are better kept out of ordinary email, and what to do when someone asks for them that way.
  • Shared workstations and screen locks. Front-desk and operatory computers are used by several people. Questions about shared logins and locking screens show where one person's access might be wider than it should be.
  • Who has access to what. Your answers about shared logins, password reuse and two-step login help the report suggest who should have their own account, and what to turn off when someone leaves.
  • Backups and restore testing. We ask if files are backed up on their own and if you ever tried restoring. The report tells you if your safety net is likely to work on a bad day.
  • Fake invoices and payment requests. Supply companies, labs and equipment vendors send many bills. We ask about suspicious requests and who can approve changes to payment details, and the report covers a two-person check.
  • Updates and old computers. Older systems that no longer get updates are a common weak spot in small offices. The report lists which devices to update or replace first.
  • Email domain protection: SPF, DKIM, DMARC and MX. These public records show whether someone could easily send email that appears to come from your practice. The report explains the results in plain English and what to ask your email provider.

Problems we hear about in this line of work

One login at the front desk

When everyone signs in as the same user, you cannot tell who did what. Separate accounts and a screen lock are low-cost changes that make a real difference.

Private details sent by regular email

A patient or a vendor asks for something to be emailed, and it goes out without a second thought. A written office habit for what is sent and how helps everyone stay consistent.

A backup nobody has tested

A drive can sit plugged in for years and still fail when needed. A quick test restore of one file tells you much more than the green light on the box.

Former staff accounts left open

Temp coverage, students and departing employees often keep access longer than intended. Listing who has an account, and removing the ones that are not needed, is a good quarterly habit.

Three quick wins you can do this week

  1. Write down every person who has a login to email and your records software, and remove anyone who no longer needs one.
  2. Turn on two-step login for office email and set every shared computer to lock after a few minutes idle.
  3. Restore one file from your backup this week to prove it works.
