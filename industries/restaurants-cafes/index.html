A security check built around how a restaurant or cafe really runs

By Alex, founder, Collar City Tech Help, Troy, NY. Last updated 2026-10-08.

Most kitchens and counters run on a mix of things: a card terminal at the register, an office PC in the back, a tablet for online orders, a few delivery-app logins, and a phone that half the staff have used at some point. Nobody planned it that way. It just grew.

Small security gaps hide in that mix. A shared tablet password may never have been changed. A Google Business Profile may still belong to the person who set it up three years ago. A seasonal hire's login may still work. None of this takes a tech degree to fix, but you have to know where to look.

What the Security Scorecard checks for restaurants and cafes

The Security Scorecard is a $79 report. You fill out a 10-minute online questionnaire and we run public checks on your business email domain. You get a graded report with a fix list. The report follows your answers, so a cafe with one owner and a part-time crew gets different advice than a restaurant with a full line and a manager.

  • Register and card terminal vs. the office PC. The questionnaire asks whether you store customer or payment information and which devices handle it. Your report says in plain terms where the back-office computer and the register should be kept apart.
  • Online ordering and delivery-app logins. We ask about password reuse, shared logins and two-step login on the accounts that bring in orders and money. The report flags which ones need a stronger password and a second step first.
  • Who owns your Google Business Profile and social accounts. Hours, photos and reviews live there, and it is often tied to one person's personal email. Your answers on social media two-step login and shared logins show up in the report with a clear next step.
  • Shared tablets and a shared Wi-Fi network. We ask if guests use the same Wi-Fi as your work devices and whether the router admin password was ever changed. The report tells you if a separate guest network is worth setting up.
  • Seasonal and part-time staff accounts. Questions about shared logins and who has access show whether old accounts might still work. You also get a short routine to follow each time someone starts or leaves.
  • Fake supplier invoices and payment changes. We ask about suspicious invoices in the past year and who can approve changing payment details. Food and supply orders make a believable cover for a fake bill, so the report covers a simple two-person check.
  • Email domain protection: SPF, DKIM, DMARC and MX. These are public records on your business email domain. We look them up and explain in plain English whether someone could easily send email pretending to be your restaurant, and what to ask your email provider.

Problems we hear about in this line of work

One tablet, one password, everyone knows it

A shared tablet at the pass or host stand is handy. If it is also logged in to your ordering system or email, anyone who picks it up can see more than they should. Locking it and limiting its access is often a ten-minute change.

The person who set it up has left

Online ordering, the Google listing and the social pages are often created by a friend, a former manager or a family member. If they leave, you may not be able to change a menu or answer a review. Start by finding out who the owner is on each account.

A convincing bill from a vendor

Restaurants pay many small suppliers, and invoices change often. A fake one that looks routine can slip through on a busy Friday. Have two people confirm any new bank details.

No backup of the files you would miss

Recipes, schedules, payroll spreadsheets and supplier lists often sit on one office PC. If it dies or gets locked by ransomware, they are gone. The report asks how you back up and whether you have ever tested a restore.

Three quick wins for this week

  1. Make a list of every account that touches orders, money or your public listing, and write down who owns each one. Keep the list in a locked drawer, not a shared spreadsheet with passwords in it.
  2. Turn on two-step login for your business email and your Google Business Profile. Both are free and take about five minutes each.
  3. Set your phones and office PC to install updates automatically, and put a screen lock on anything that stays at the counter.
